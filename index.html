DriveReport

Astana

Growing up in Astana, I often experienced problems with public transportation. In winter, buses were overcrowded. Road repairs changed bus routes and disrupted my plans. In spring, some streets flooded almost knee-deep, and in summer, some buses broke down because their engines overheated.

I began wondering whether these problems could be solved and whether people would be willing to report them. I started by mapping Astana’s bus routes, marking congested streets, and trying to understand how the network was distributed. I wanted to see how individual road problems could affect people across the city.

Then I tried to build a Telegram bot. After a lot of thinking and two trial versions, I realized that I did not yet have the skills and tools to continue in that direction. But I did not want to give up on the idea. Before moving on to a website, I wrote an article titled “Gamification Framework for Driver Network: A New Method for Real-Time Data Gathering and Adaptive Public Transportation Optimization.” In it, I explored how gamification in a driver network might help gather real-time data and improve public transportation.

Later, I created a website with an incident map and a form for submitting reports with photos. It helped me turn an idea into something I could show and discuss. But I began to understand that liking the idea of an app does not mean someone will actually use it. And if people never learn what happened after submitting a report, they may not report again.

To find out whether others recognized the same problems, I surveyed 42 people. In my survey, 71.4% said they used public transportation every day. Another 57.1% said they did not report problems because they doubted anything would be fixed. Aggressive driving was named by 40.5%, and blocked bus stops by 33.3%. Meanwhile, 57.2% rated the app idea 4 or 5 out of 5. These responses encouraged me to keep working, but I know a survey of 42 people cannot represent everyone in Astana.

As I explored the idea, I realized that DriveReport would not exist in a vacuum. 2GIS and Yandex Maps already show road events reported by users. Waze and Google Maps also let drivers report crashes and other road problems. So I do not want to build another general map or navigation app. I want to focus on a narrower problem in Astana: road situations that can disrupt bus travel. The idea’s strength is its narrow focus: connecting drivers’ observations with a clear process for submitting, reviewing, and following up on reports. I see DriveReport as a possible complement to existing maps, not a replacement for them.

While developing DriveReport, I also began thinking about trustworthy reports and privacy. I explored comparing a photo’s location data with the reported incident location. But I realized that missing GPS data does not necessarily mean a report is false; some phones simply do not save it. Users should understand the limits of verification rather than face a quick assumption. I also developed the idea of “Silent Witness,” allowing someone to report a problem anonymously or under a pseudonym.

I attended Alem.ai Demo Day for one day. It gave me a chance to look at my project from a different perspective and ask whether I was testing my assumptions and listening enough to the people I hoped to serve. Along the way, I taught myself web development, learned about photo data, and explored how an app might work without an internet connection.

I do not claim that DriveReport has already solved Astana’s transportation problems. I still need to test the idea with drivers, keep learning, and find people who can help me work toward a pilot. It began with ordinary trips and one question: why is it so hard to report problems everyone can see? Now I am trying to find an answer by turning that question into a project.
